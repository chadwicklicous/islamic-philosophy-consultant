# The Active Intellect and the Soul
## A Comparative Study of the Islamic Philosophers and St. Thomas Aquinas

## Abstract

This essay asks a question that sits at the very heart of the philosophy of mind: **what is the active intellect, and what does it mean for the soul?** The Islamic philosophers — al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and above all Avicenna — and the Christian philosopher St. Thomas Aquinas inherited the same Greek problem (how does the human mind pass from not-knowing to knowing?) and gave sharply different answers. For Avicenna and al-Farabi, the active intellect is **one separate, eternal substance** that illuminates all human souls from outside. For Aquinas, it is **a power of each individual human soul** — the "spark of reason" that is yours, not borrowed. This essay walks through that difference in two movements. First, it establishes the *metaphysics of the soul* — how the soul exists, how it is individuated, and how it survives death — in both traditions. Second, it explores the *epistemology* — what the active intellect does, how the soul knows, and the process by which it attains truth. Throughout, the essay contrasts the two frames to make visible the deepest consequence: whether the light by which we know is *shared* or *ours*, and what that means for the individual immortality of the soul.

# Chapter 1: The Metaphysics of the Soul

## 1.1 The question of how the soul exists

Before we can say anything about the active intellect, we have to ask a more basic question: **what is the soul, and how does it exist?** This is not an idle preliminary — the answer determines everything downstream. If the soul is a *body* or a *power of a body*, then it dies with the body, and the whole question of what happens to our knowing after death collapses. If the soul is a *separate substance* — something that has its own being, not borrowed from matter — then it can survive the body, and the question of how it knows becomes urgent.

Both the Islamic philosophers and Aquinas agree on the first, decisive point: **the human soul is not a body, and it is not a power of a body.** It is a substance that has its own being. But they arrive at this conclusion through different routes, and the difference matters.

## 1.2 The soul as a substance with two faces

Avicenna gives us the most vivid image of the soul in the *al-Najat* (The Deliverance). The rational soul, he says, is a single substance with **two faces** — one turned toward the body, one turned toward the higher principles:

> "وكأن للنفس منا وجهين وجه إلى البدن ويجب أن يكون هذا الوجه غير قابل البتة أثراً من جنس مقتضى طبيعة البدن ووجه إلى المبادئ العالية ويجب أن يكون هذا الوجه دائم القبول عما هناك"
> — *"The soul has two faces: a face toward the body, which must not be affected by anything of the body's nature, and a face toward the higher principles, which is always receiving from there."*

Let me unpack this image, because it carries the whole metaphysics. The soul is **one** thing, but it stands in two relations. In its relation to the body, it is the *form* and *governor* — it animates the body, directs it, and uses it as an instrument. But in its relation to what is above it — the separate intellects and ultimately the First Truth — it is a *receiver*, always open to the light that comes from beyond. The soul is not trapped in the body; it straddles the boundary between the material and the immaterial.

This is why Avicenna insists the soul is **not a body and not a bodily power**. If it were, it could not do what it manifestly does — know itself, and know that it knows. A bodily organ can sense what is outside it, but it cannot turn back on itself. The eye sees colors but does not see itself seeing. The soul, by contrast, knows itself and knows its own act of knowing. Avicenna makes this the proof that the intellective power operates **by its own essence, not by a bodily organ**:

> "لو كانت تعقل بالآلة الجسدانية... لكان يجب أن لا تعقل ذاتها... لكنها تعقل ذاتها وآلتها وأنها عقلت فإذاً إنما تعقل بذاتها لا بالآلة"
> — *"If the intellective power knew by a bodily organ, it would not know itself... but it knows itself and its organ and that it has known — so it knows by its own essence, not by an organ."*

The point is subtle but crucial. The soul's ability to *reflect on itself* — to know that it knows — is the signature of an immaterial substance. Matter cannot do this. So the soul, being able to do it, is not material.

## 1.3 The two powers of the soul: practical and theoretical

Avicenna divides the rational soul's powers into two: the **practical intellect** (العقل العملي) and the **theoretical intellect** (العقل النظري). Let me define these, because they will matter throughout.

The **practical intellect** is the soul's power turned toward action. It governs the body, deliberates about what to do, and produces the moral virtues. It is the power by which we decide, choose, and act well. It is, in a sense, the soul's "manager" — the power that keeps the passions in check and directs the body toward the good.

The **theoretical intellect** is the soul's power turned toward *knowing*. It is the power by which the soul receives the universal forms — the essences of things abstracted from matter. This is the power that makes the soul, in Avicenna's phrase, "an intellect in act." And it is this theoretical intellect that is the *essence* of the soul — the part that is most truly itself.

Al-Farabi, in his *'Uyun al-Masa'il* (The Sources of the Questions), makes the same distinction and adds a crucial point: the theoretical intellect is what the soul *is*, and it can pass through stages of actualization:

> "ومن قوى النفس العقل العلمي وهو الذى جوهر النفس ويصير جوهراً عقلياً بالفعل ولهذا العقل مراتب يكون مرة عقلا هيولانياً ومرة عقل"
> — *"Among the soul's powers is the scientific intellect, which is the soul's essence, and it becomes an intellect in act. This intellect has stages: sometimes it is a material [potential] intellect, sometimes an intellect [in act]."*

So the soul is not born knowing. It begins as a **potential intellect** — a blank, a capacity — and it becomes an **actual intellect** by acquiring the forms of things. This is the process we will explore in Chapter 2.

## 1.4 The individuation of the soul

Now we come to a question that will prove decisive: **what makes one soul different from another?** If all human souls are the same kind of thing — immaterial substances with the same essence — what individuates them? Why is my soul *mine* and not yours?

Here the two traditions diverge, and the divergence is the seed of everything that follows.

**For Avicenna, the soul is individuated by its relation to a particular body.** The soul is not individuated by anything in itself — as an immaterial substance, it has no matter to distinguish it. What makes each soul particular is *which body it is joined to*. The body is the soul's "instrument and kingdom," and the soul is individuated by the particular body it governs. From the *al-Najat*:

> "نعم البدن والمزاج علة بالعرض للنفس فإنه إذا حدثت مادة بدن يصلح أن يكون آلة النفس ومملكة لها أحدثت العلل المفارقة النفس الجزئية"
> — *"The body and temperament are an accidental cause of the soul: when a body-matter arises fit to be the soul's instrument and kingdom, the separate causes bring about the particular soul."*

This is a striking claim. The soul is a separate substance, but it is *made particular* by the body it is tied to. The body does not cause the soul's *existence* (that comes from the separate causes), but it causes the soul's *particularity*. Without a body, there would be no "this soul" as opposed to "that soul."

**For Aquinas, the soul is individuated by its own subsistent form.** Aquinas agrees the soul is immaterial and subsistent, but he draws a different conclusion about individuation. Because the soul is a *form that has being per se* — a subsistent form — it is individuated by being *this* form, not by being joined to *this* body. The soul is not individuated by the body; it is a particular substance in its own right. This is why, as we shall see, the soul survives death as an individual.

The difference is subtle but profound. For Avicenna, the soul's individuality is *borrowed* from the body — it is a relation. For Aquinas, the soul's individuality is *intrinsic* — it is a substance. And this difference will determine what happens to the soul — and to its knowing — after death.

## 1.5 The immortality of the soul

Both traditions hold that the soul survives death. But they ground this in different ways, and the difference matters for the question of the active intellect.

**Avicenna** argues at length that the soul does not die with the body. His argument is a careful elimination of the possible ways the soul could depend on the body. The soul cannot depend on the body as an *effect* depends on its *cause* — for the body, being a body, cannot cause an immaterial substance. The soul cannot depend on the body as a *form* depends on its *matter* — for the soul is not "impressed" in the body. The only relation is an *accidental* one: the body is the soul's instrument, and when the instrument is destroyed, the soul does not perish with it. From the *al-Najat*:

> "ونقول أنها لا تموت بموت البدن ولا تقبل الفساد أصلاً... ليس تعلق النفس بالبدن تعلق معلول بعلة ذاتية - نعم البدن والمزاج علة بالعرض للنفس"
> — *"We say that it does not die with the death of the body, nor does it admit corruption at all... The soul's relation to the body is not the relation of an effect to an essential cause — rather, the body and temperament are an accidental cause of the soul."*

So for Avicenna, the soul survives death because it is a **separate substance** that only *uses* the body as an instrument. When the instrument breaks, the substance remains.

**Aquinas** argues the same conclusion but grounds it in the soul's *subsistence*. In the *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 75, a. 6, he gives the classic proof:

> "necesse est dicere animam humanam, quam dicimus intellectivum principium, esse incorruptibilem... Esse autem per se convenit formae, quae est actus... Impossibile est autem quod forma separetur a seipsa. Unde impossibile est quod forma subsistens desinat esse."
> — *"It is necessary to say that the human soul, which we call the intellective principle, is incorruptible... Being per se belongs to the form, which is act... It is impossible that a form be separated from itself. Hence it is impossible that a subsistent form cease to be."*

The argument is elegant. The soul is a *form* — it is the act of a body. But unlike the forms of animals and plants, the human soul is a *subsistent* form: it has being in itself, not merely in the composite. And being per se belongs to a form that is act. Since the soul is a subsistent form, it cannot be corrupted — for corruption is the separation of form from matter, and a form that subsists cannot be separated from itself.

So both traditions affirm the immortality of the soul. But note the difference in *ground*: for Avicenna, the soul survives because it is a separate substance that uses the body; for Aquinas, the soul survives because it is a subsistent form whose being is intrinsic. This difference will become decisive when we ask what happens to the *active intellect* after death.

## 1.6 The fate of the soul after death

What happens to the soul when it leaves the body? Here the two traditions converge on a striking point, and it sets up the whole of Chapter 2.

**Avicenna** holds that the soul's fate after death is determined by what it has *become* in this life. The soul that has perfected its theoretical intellect — that has become an intellect in act, united to the active intellect — enjoys intellectual happiness, which is the *approach to the First Truth*. The soul that has not is deprived. From the *al-Najat* (the return of souls):

> "المعاد منه مقبول من الشرع... ومنه ما هو مدرك بالعقل والقياس البرهاني وقد صدقته النبوة وهو السعادة والشقاوة الثابتتان بالمقاييس اللتان للأنفس"
> — *"The return (ma'ad) is partly accepted from the Law... and partly known by reason and demonstrative syllogism, which prophecy confirms — namely the happiness and misery established by demonstration, which belong to the souls."*

And the highest happiness is defined precisely:

> "السعادة التي هي مقاربة الحق الأول"
> — *"the happiness which is the approach to the First Truth."*

So for Avicenna, the soul's post-mortem state is a matter of *intellectual attainment*. The soul that has become an intellect in act is united to the active intellect and approaches the First Truth; the soul that has not is cut off.

**Aquinas** agrees that the separated soul retains its intellective power, but he adds a crucial qualification: the separated soul cannot *fully* know without the body, because the human intellect naturally knows through phantasms (images drawn from the senses). This is why Aquinas holds that the *resurrection* of the body is needed for the soul's full operation. The soul survives, but its natural mode of knowing is impeded without the body.

This sets up the central question of Chapter 2: **what is the active intellect, and how does the soul know?**

# Chapter 2: The Active Intellect and the Epistemology of Truth

## 2.1 The problem: how does the mind pass from not-knowing to knowing?

We begin with a puzzle that every philosopher of mind must face. The human mind, at birth, does not know. It is a *capacity*, a blank. Yet somehow it comes to know — to grasp the universal essences of things, to understand causes, to attain truth. How does this happen?

The puzzle is sharper than it first appears. Nothing passes from potency to act by itself. A seed does not become a tree without soil and sun; a child does not become a man without nourishment and teaching. So the mind, which begins in potency, must be *actualized* by something already in act. The question is: **what actualizes the mind?**

The answer the Islamic philosophers and Aquinas both give — inheriting it from Aristotle — is that there is a power called the **active intellect** (العقل الفعال / *intellectus agens*). But they disagree profoundly about *what* this active intellect is. And that disagreement is the subject of this chapter.

## 2.2 What is the active intellect? — defining the term

Let me define the term carefully, because it is the hinge of the whole essay.

The human mind has two aspects, which the tradition calls the **possible intellect** and the **active intellect**.

The **possible intellect** (also called the *material* or *potential* intellect) is the mind's *capacity to receive* — the blank slate, the power to become all things by knowing them. It is passive: it receives the forms of things.

The **active intellect** is the mind's *power to abstract* — the power that takes the universal form out of the particular, material thing and makes it actually intelligible. It is active: it does the work of making things knowable.

Here is the crucial question: **is the active intellect a power of each individual soul, or is it one separate substance shared by all?**

## 2.3 The Islamic answer: one separate active intellect

**Avicenna** argues that the active intellect is **one, separate, immaterial substance** — an intellect in act by its essence, which illuminates all human souls. His argument is a regress-argument. The theoretical power of the soul cannot pass from potency to act by itself; something already in act must actualize it. That something cannot itself be in potency (or the regress would go to infinity). So there must be something that is *an intellect by its essence* — and this is the active intellect. From the *al-Najat*:

> "فإذاً ههنا شيء يفيد النفس ويطبع فيها من جوهر صور المعقولات فذات هذا الشيء لا محالة عنده صور المعقولات وهذا الشيء إذاً بذاته عقل ولو كان بالقوة عقلاً لامتد الأمر إلى غير نهاية وهذا محال أو وقف عند شيء هو بجوهره عقل"
> — *"There is here something that benefits the soul and imprints in it the forms of the intelligibles... If it were an intellect in potency, the matter would extend to infinity, which is impossible — so it must stop at something that is an intellect by its essence."*

So for Avicenna, the active intellect is **one** — there is a single cosmic intellect that actualizes all human minds. It is not *in* any soul; it is *above* all souls, a separate substance in the hierarchy of intellects that descends from the First Truth.

**Al-Farabi** agrees, and he gives the most famous image of the active intellect's work — the **sun/light analogy**. Just as the sun actualizes sight and makes colors visible, so the active intellect actualizes the mind and makes the intelligibles intelligible. From his *Ma'ani al-'Aql* (The Meanings of the Intellect):

> "ونسبة العقل الفعال الى العقل الذي بالقوة كنسبة الشمس الى العين التي هي بصر بالقوة ما دامت في الظلمة"
> — *"The active intellect's relation to the intellect in potency is like the sun's relation to the eye that is sight in potency while in darkness."*

The image is powerful. The eye, in darkness, is *potentially* seeing — it has the capacity, but nothing is visible. The sun does two things: it makes the eye actually seeing, and it makes colors actually visible. So the active intellect does two things: it makes the mind actually knowing, and it makes the intelligibles actually knowable. It is the light by which we see.

## 2.4 What the active intellect does

So what, precisely, does the active intellect do? Avicenna gives the fullest account. The active intellect **pours out a power** that flows to the imagined things and makes them actually intelligible, and it makes the potential intellect actually intellective. From the *al-Najat*:

> "ونسبة هذا الشيء إلى أنفسنا التي هي بالقوة عقل وإلى المعقولات التي هي بالقوة معقولات نسبة الشمس إلى أبصارنا... فإنها إذا اتصل بالمرئيات بالقوة منها ذلك الأثر وهو الشعاع عادت مرئيات بالفعل وعاد البصر رائياً بالفعل فكذلك هذا العقل الفعال يفيض منه قوة تسيح إلى الأشياء المتخيلة التي هي بالقوة معقولة لتجعلها معقولة بالفعل"
> — *"The relation of this thing to our souls (intellect in potency) and to the intelligibles (intelligible in potency) is like the sun's relation to our sight... just as the ray makes visible things actually visible and sight actually seeing, so the active intellect pours out a power that flows to the imagined things, making them actually intelligible."*

Let me unpack this. The active intellect does not *create* the intelligibles from nothing. It *actualizes* what is potentially intelligible. The forms of things are already there, in the material world, but they are not yet *known*. The active intellect, by its light, makes them knowable — it abstracts the universal from the particular, the form from the matter. And in doing so, it actualizes the mind itself, which passes from potential to actual knowing.

This is the epistemology of the Islamic philosophers: **knowing is the actualization of the potential intellect by the active intellect.** The light is shared; the mind is illuminated from above.

## 2.5 How the soul knows: the epistemological process

Now we come to the heart of the matter: **how does the soul actually come to know?** Avicenna gives a precise account of the process, and it is worth walking through step by step.

The soul knows by **acquiring the middle term of a syllogism**. This is the key technical idea. In Aristotelian logic, a syllogism has two premises and a conclusion. The "middle term" is the link that connects the two premises and makes the conclusion certain. For example: *All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal.* The middle term is "man" — it is what connects Socrates to mortality. To *know* something, in the strong sense, is to grasp the middle term — the cause that makes the conclusion true.

Avicenna says the soul acquires this middle term in **two ways**: by **intuition** (حدس) and by **teaching** (تعليم). From the *al-Najat*:

> "الأمور المعقولة التي يتوصل إلى اكتسابها إنما تكتسب بحصول الحد الأوسط في القياس - وهذا الحد الأوسط قد يحصل ضربين من الحصول فتارة يحصل بالحدس والحدس فعل للذهن يستنبط به بذاته الحد الأوسط والذكاء قوة الحدس وتارة يحصل بالتعليم ومبادئ التعليم الحدس"
> — *"The intelligible things that are acquired are acquired by the attainment of the middle term in the syllogism. This middle term is attained in two ways: sometimes by intuition (ḥads) — intuition is an act of the mind by which it discovers the middle term by itself, and intelligence is the power of intuition — and sometimes by teaching, and the principles of teaching are intuition."*

Let me unpack this, because it is the epistemological process in miniature.

**First, intuition (حدس).** This is the mind's own power of discovery. When the mind is confronted with a problem, it can *see* the middle term — the connecting link — by its own act. This is not a passive reception; it is an active grasping. The mind "intuits" the cause that makes the conclusion certain. And this power of intuition is what we call *intelligence* (الذكاء) — the quickness of the mind in finding the middle term.

**Second, teaching (تعليم).** This is how most of us learn. We are told the middle term by a teacher. But — and this is the crucial point — *the principles of teaching are intuition*. Every teacher, at some point, discovered the middle term by their own intuition and then passed it on. So all knowledge ultimately rests on someone's intuition. The chain of teaching terminates in an act of discovery.

So the epistemological process is: the soul, as potential intellect, is actualized by the active intellect; it acquires the middle term of a syllogism, either by its own intuition or by teaching; and in doing so, it passes from potential to actual knowing.

## 2.6 The highest knowledge: the holy intellect and the First Truth

Avicenna describes the **highest degree** of this capacity — the **holy intellect** (العقل القدسي). This is the point at which intuition is so strong that the soul barely needs the active intellect's help; it seems to know everything from itself. From the *al-Najat*:

> "هذا الاستعداد قد يشتد في بعض الناس حتى لا يحتاج في أن يتصل بالعقل الفعال إلى كبير شيء... بل كأنه يعرف كل شيء من نفسه - وهذه الدرجة أعلى درجات هذا الاستعداد"
> — *"This disposition may intensify in some people until they need little to connect with the active intellect... as if they know everything from themselves — and this is the highest degree of this disposition."*

This is the intellect of the prophet, the one who receives knowledge directly, without teaching. It is the summit of the epistemological ladder.

And the goal of all this knowing is the **First Truth** — God, the cause of all truth. Al-Kindi, in his *On First Philosophy*, grounds the whole enterprise in this:

> "أشرف الفلسفة وأعلاها مرتبة الفلسفة الأولى: أعني علم الحق الأول الذي هو علة كل حق... لأن علم العلة أشرف من علم المعلول"
> — *"The noblest philosophy and highest rank is First Philosophy: the science of the First Truth, which is the cause of every truth... for the knowledge of the cause is nobler than the knowledge of the effect."*

So the epistemological process has a *telos*: the soul ascends from potential intellect to actual intellect, from intuition to the holy intellect, and its ultimate object is the First Truth, the cause of all truth.

## 2.7 The Thomistic answer: the active intellect as a power of the soul

Now we turn to Aquinas, and here the divergence becomes decisive. **Aquinas rejects the separate active intellect.** He argues, against Avicenna and Averroes, that the active intellect is **a power of each individual human soul** — not a separate substance shared by all.

His argument is in the *Contra Gentiles* II, 76, whose very title states the conclusion:

> "Quod intellectus agens non sit substantia separata, sed aliquid animae"
> — *"That the active intellect is not a separate substance, but something of the soul."*

Aquinas gives several arguments. The most powerful is the **proportionality argument**. The active intellect and the possible intellect must be *proportioned* to each other — the agent must be suited to its patient. But the possible intellect is a part of the soul. Therefore the active intellect, which acts on the possible intellect, must also be a part of the soul:

> "Cum enim agens et recipiens sint proportionata, oportet quod unicuique passivo respondeat proprium activum. Intellectus autem possibilis comparatur ad agentem ut proprium passivum... Si igitur intellectus possibilis est aliquid animae humanae... et intellectus agens erit etiam eiusmodi, et non erit unus omnium."
> — *"For since the agent and the recipient are proportioned, each passive thing must have its own proper active thing. The possible intellect is related to the agent as its proper patient... If therefore the possible intellect is something of the human soul... the active intellect will also be of this kind, and will not be one for all."*

And he draws the Platonic parallel — if the active intellect were separate, it would be like Plato's separate Ideas, which Aristotle rejected:

> "Plato posuit scientiam in nobis causari ab ideis, quas ponebat esse quasdam substantias separatas... Si igitur intellectus agens esset quaedam substantia separata, nulla esset vel modica differentia inter opinionem istam et Platonicam a philosopho improbatam."
> — *"Plato held that our knowledge is caused by the Ideas, which he held to be separate substances... If the active intellect were a separate substance, there would be no or little difference between this opinion and the Platonic one rejected by the Philosopher."*

So for Aquinas, the active intellect is **the soul's own power of abstraction** — the "spark of reason" that is yours, not borrowed. It is the power by which the soul abstracts the universal from the particular, and it is *in* the soul.

## 2.8 The implications of a shared active intellect

Now we come to the deepest question of the essay: **what are the implications of a shared active intellect?**

Aquinas saw the consequences with complete clarity, and he drew them out ruthlessly. If the active intellect is one separate substance shared by all men, then:

**First, all men would understand alike.** The same separate agent would act on all phantasms equally. The learned and the ignorant, the wise and the foolish, would all be illuminated the same way. From the *Contra Gentiles* II, 76:

> "Comparatio substantiae separatae ad omnia phantasmata quae sunt in quibuscumque hominibus, est una... Uterque ergo similiter intelliget."
> — *"The relation of the separate substance to all the phantasms in all men is one... Therefore both [the learned and the ignorant] will understand alike."*

**Second, understanding would not be in our power.** If the active intellect's action were continuous (as a separate substance's would be), then we would always be understanding — or never. The act of intellection would not be something we do; it would be something done to us. From the same chapter:

> "Si intellectus agens est quaedam substantia separata, oportet quod eius actio sit continua... Ergo oportet quod vel semper intelligamus; vel quod non sit in potestate nostra actu intelligere."
> — *"If the active intellect is a separate substance, its action must be continuous... Therefore either we always understand, or it is not in our power to understand in act."*

These are devastating implications. A shared active intellect would destroy the **individuality of understanding** (we would all know the same things the same way) and the **freedom of the act of intellection** (we would not be the agents of our own knowing). For Aquinas, this is a reductio ad absurdum.

**Aquinas's own solution** is to say that even if there is a higher separate intellect (God, or the angels), the soul must have its **own participated power** of abstraction. He uses the analogy of the sun and generation: the sun does not generate man alone; man has his own generative power. From the *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 79, a. 4:

> "non enim solus sol generat hominem, sed est in homine virtus generativa hominis"
> — *"the sun alone does not generate man, but there is in man a generative power of man."*

So the higher intellect may illuminate, but the soul has its own active power. The light is not merely borrowed; it is *participated* — the soul has a share of it that is its own.

## 2.9 Does the active intellect individuate the soul?

This brings us to the question you asked: **does the active intellect individuate the soul?**

**In Aquinas — yes, decisively.** Because the active intellect is a power of the individual soul, and the soul is a subsistent form that has being per se, the soul is individuated and incorruptible. The "spark of reason" is not a borrowed light from a separate substance; it is the soul's own power. This is why the soul survives death as an individual — because the active intellect is *in* it, and the soul is a subsistent form. The active intellect individuates the soul because it is part of the soul.

**In Avicenna — no.** The soul is individuated by its relation to a particular body, not by the active intellect. The active intellect is the same for all; what differs is the body each soul is joined to. The soul's individuality is *borrowed* from the body, not from the active intellect.

This is the deepest divergence. For Aquinas, the light by which we know is *ours* — it individuates us and is why we survive death as *ourselves*. For Avicenna, the light is *shared* — it is the same sun for all, and what individuates us is not the light but the body we happened to be joined to.

## 2.10 How does the active intellect exist after death?

Finally, we come to the question of the active intellect's existence after death.

**In Aquinas:** the active intellect, being a power of the soul, **survives with the soul**. It is part of the incorruptible subsistent form. The soul retains its active intellect after death, though its operation is impeded without the body (since the human intellect naturally knows through phantasms). This is why Aquinas holds that the resurrection of the body is needed for the soul's full operation.

**In Avicenna:** the active intellect is **separate and eternal** — it never "dies" because it was never in the body. It exists always, in act, as one of the separate intellects. What changes at death is the **soul's relation to it**. The soul that has perfected its theoretical intellect — that has become an intellect in act, an "acquired intellect" in Farabi's terms — is united to the active intellect and enjoys intellectual happiness, the approach to the First Truth. The soul that has not is cut off.

So the answer to "how does the active intellect exist after death" is different in the two traditions:

- **For Aquinas**, the active intellect exists after death *in the soul* — it is part of the soul's own incorruptible substance.
- **For Avicenna**, the active intellect exists after death *separately* — it always existed separately, and the soul's fate is its *connection to* or *separation from* it.

## 2.11 The synthesis: shared light or own spark?

We have traced the active intellect through two traditions, and the divergence is now clear. Let me state it as starkly as possible.

| | **Aquinas** | **Avicenna / al-Farabi** |
|---|---|---|
| **The active intellect** | A power of each individual soul | One separate, eternal substance |
| **Individuation of the soul** | By the soul's own subsistent form (the active intellect is *in* it) | By the soul's relation to a particular body |
| **After death** | The active intellect survives *with* the soul (incorruptible form) | The active intellect always exists separately; the soul's fate is its *connection to* or *separation from* it |
| **Why we know** | The soul's own power abstracts universals (with a higher intellect's help) | The separate active intellect illuminates the soul |
| **The risk Aquinas sees** | — | If shared, all would understand alike; understanding wouldn't be in our power |

The deepest point is this. For Aquinas, the "spark of reason" is **yours** — it individuates you and is why you survive death as *you*. For Avicenna, the light is **shared** — it is the same sun for all, and what individuates you is not the light but the body you happened to be joined to.

This is why Aquinas's critique of the shared active intellect is so pointed. It threatens the very **individuality of the knowing subject** — and with it, the **individual immortality of the soul**. If the light by which we know is shared, then what is *mine* about my knowing? And if my knowing is not mine, then what is *mine* about my soul?

The two traditions thus give two answers to the same question — *what is the active intellect?* — and the answers carry the whole weight of their respective anthropologies. For the Islamic philosophers, the soul is a separate substance illuminated from above, individuated by its body, and its immortality is the immortality of a substance that uses the body. For Aquinas, the soul is a subsistent form with its own active power, individuated by its own being, and its immortality is the immortality of a form that cannot be separated from itself. The active intellect is the hinge on which both turn — and the difference between a shared light and an own spark is the difference between two visions of what it means to be a knowing, immortal soul.
